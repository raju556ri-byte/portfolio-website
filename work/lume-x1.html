LUME-X1 Liquid Rocket Engine

A pressure-fed ethanol/GOX engine, designed, built and static-fired by a four-person final-year team at Pulchowk Campus, Tribhuvan University.

My role

I led the propulsion design, simulation and fabrication of LUME-X1 in a team of four with Sandesh Kunwar, Prabin Bhattarai and Shubham Ranabhat. My work ran from NASA CEA and CFD analysis through machining the injector, chamber and nozzle to integration and static-fire testing.

Design

LUME-X1 burns ethanol with gaseous oxygen, both fed by gas pressure with no turbopumps. The design targets were 130 N of thrust at a chamber pressure of 150 psi and an oxidizer-to-fuel ratio of 1.4. The combustion chamber was sized for a characteristic length of 1.2 m with a contraction ratio of 7.2. An unlike-doublet impinging injector mixes the propellants, a phenolic ablative liner protects the chamber, and a stainless steel nozzle completes the stack.

Build and test setup

The injector, chamber and nozzle were machined and assembled as one bolted stack, clamped with threaded rods so the thrust passes straight into a load cell. Seals follow ARP 1232 practice using Buna-N O-rings. The test stand carries a regulator, oxygen and nitrogen supplies, a pressure transducer, a thermocouple and a high-speed camera.

Simulation

CFD of the nozzle flow predicted an under-expanded supersonic jet with shock diamonds, with the Mach number peaking near 2. In the thesis, the oscillating pattern of the simulated Mach number along the centreline matches the pattern of light intensity along the plume in the exhaust photographs.

Results

The first hot fire ran successfully, with a peak thrust of 99.6 N over a 2.7 s burn. Tests 2 and 3 reached 121.5 N and 112.8 N and burned for 4.5 s and 5.1 s. Chamber pressure was only measured from Test 4 onward, so combustion efficiency is reported for Tests 4 and 5.

Limits and next steps

Measured thrust stayed below the 130 N design target. The thesis puts this down to incomplete combustion and losses in the injector and nozzle, which an ideal-conditions model does not include. The engine uses an uncooled steel chamber, which limits burn time. The next steps are longer burns, lower engine weight, more thrust and regenerative cooling.
