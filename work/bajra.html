My contribution to my seniors’ final-year project · 2025

Project Bajra: Motor Casing and Graphite Nozzle

Bajra is an active fin-controlled rocket built by my seniors as their final-year project. I fabricated its lightweight solid rocket motor, precision-machined the graphite nozzle, prepared the propellant and got the rocket ready on the launch stand, to strict weight and performance limits for flight testing.

The project

Bajra is an integrated control system for stabilizing and guiding a rocket with active fins. A flight computer reads an IMU and GPS and runs a PID controller that moves servo-driven fins. The senior team developed the control system, the flight computer and the fin mechanism. In their tests the rocket followed a 15° pitch command in a wind tunnel with no overshoot, and in a flight test the roll control held the roll angle to about 5° of error.

My part: the motor casing

The rocket needed a casing that was light and could take the heat of the burn. I made it from 450 GSM fiberglass composite with polyester resin, laid up on a 32 mm pipe. The pipe was wrapped in printing paper and then cello tape so the cured casing would slide off. The motor dimensions came from OpenMotor simulations.

My part: the graphite nozzle

Choosing the nozzle material took several tries, because the weight limit ruled out heavy materials and the hot exhaust eroded most of the lighter ones. The graphite nozzle was the one chosen for flight tests. It was designed with OpenMotor and nozzle equations, checked in ANSYS, and machined on a lathe. Silicone grease, a flame-resistant lubricant, helped limit erosion.

My part: propellant and launch day

I also prepared the propellant. The grains were cast from a hot potassium nitrate and sugar mixture, stirred in a pan and poured into moulds to make the three grains that load into the casing. On launch day I prepared the rocket on its stand for flight.

Testing and flight

The senior team tested three propellants, a potassium nitrate and sugar mix (KNSU), black powder and an epoxy-based composite. They chose KNSU for its burn rate, its gentle effect on the composite motor and its ease of preparation. The three-grain motor, with 50 mm grains, 30 mm wide with a 10 mm core, was thrust-tested twice on a stand, and both tests were close to the simulation. In one test the motor sat inside the servo housing to check that the housing would survive the heat, and the housing was undamaged.

The rocket then flew twice, once with roll control and once without it. With the control on, the roll angle stayed within about 5° of error, and the recovery system fired and opened the parachute.
