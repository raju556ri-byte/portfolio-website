Shock Polar Analysis at Mach 6

A Python shock polar solver, checked against ANSYS CFD, for two dual-wedge shock interactions at Mach 6. A 10°/15° pair crosses regularly, and a 35°/35° pair needs a Mach stem.

The question

When two oblique shocks from opposite wedges meet, what happens depends on whether their secondary shock polars intersect. If they do, the shocks cross and refract, leaving a single slip line. If they do not, the flow cannot be matched with oblique shocks alone, and a near-normal Mach stem forms. This matters for inlet design, because the Mach stem costs a lot of total pressure.

Method

I solved the θ–β–M relation in Python, using a root finder for the weak-shock angle at each wedge deflection. Normal-shock relations then gave the downstream Mach number and pressure ratio. The primary polar comes from sweeping the shock angle from the Mach angle (9.59° at Mach 6) up to 90°. The secondary polars are drawn from the downstream states, centred at (θ1, P2/P∞) and (−θ2, P3/P∞). Both cases use γ = 1.4. The code is in a shared Google Drive folder →

In the first case the shocks are weak and the flow stays highly supersonic, so the secondary polars are large and cross. In the second case each 35° shock slows the flow to near Mach 1.9. A flow at that Mach number can only be turned back by about 21°, but each polar would need 35° to reach the centreline, so the secondary polars cannot meet.

The transition between the two types comes from the same trade: more deflection makes the primary shock stronger, which shrinks the secondary polar and widens the gap it has to bridge. At Mach 6 and γ = 1.4 the detachment limit is about 42.4°, so 35° is an attached shock that still produces a Mach stem.

What I took from it

By shock strength, Type II should lose far more total pressure than Type I. The primary shocks are already strong (normal Mach component 4.46), and the near-normal Mach stem adds to the loss. In an inlet, the wedge angles should be chosen to stay in the regular regime.
