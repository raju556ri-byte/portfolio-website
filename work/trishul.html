Project Trishul: A Solid-Fuel Rocket Flown to 2.6 km

A two-metre solid-fuel rocket designed, built and flown by Team SRB at Pulchowk Campus, with onboard telemetry, a dual-parachute recovery system and a custom launch controller.

My role

I led propulsion, structure and fabrication for Team SRB’s Trishul rocket. The team was organized into structures, propulsion, aerodynamics and avionics units, and the project ran from OpenRocket simulation through motor testing and fabrication to flight.

Design and simulation

The goal was a 3,000 m apogee. Team SRB modelled the rocket in OpenRocket, about 2 m long, with a candidate solid motor. The first design predicted an apogee of 3,086 m. After the motor was tested on the ground and every part was fabricated and weighed, the model was updated with the real masses and the tested motor, and the predicted apogee fell to 2,577 m. The real rocket and motor were heavier and gave less impulse than the first design.

Motor static tests

The motor is an L-class solid motor using a sugar-based propellant (the OpenRocket model calls it a dextrose engine). The team tested it twice on a static stand before building the rocket around it. In the second test, careful weighing and casting of the propellant raised the impulse, peak thrust and burn time compared with the first.

Airframe, avionics and recovery

  • Structure: the body tube, couplers, nose cone and fins are fiberglass and polyester resin, made through repeated trials with different materials and molds. The fins were checked for flutter.
  • Aerodynamics: the body was sanded smooth, the fin joints were filleted using a laser-cut jig, and the fin edges were chamfered to a diamond wedge profile to reduce drag.
  • Avionics: a Teensy 4.1 flight computer logged data from a BMP180 barometer and an MPU6050 motion sensor, with GPS, LoRa telemetry and SD card storage. The barometer detects apogee.
  • Recovery: a dual-parachute system, deployed by an ejection charge through a shock cord.
  • Ground equipment: a custom launch stand and a launch controller with dual-switch ignition and a continuity check.

Flight results

On 4 January 2025 Trishul flew vertically with a 3,000 m target and reached an apogee of 2,612 m with stable flight. The ejection charge fired at the intended point, the parachute opened, and the rocket landed with only minor structural stress. All the electronics and the flight data were recovered. That is about 87% of the target altitude, and the report describes the project as almost meeting its main goals.

Altitude was logged by the onboard BMP180 barometer, so it is a pressure-based estimate, not a tracked GPS altitude. The real flight followed the simulated curve closely: a clean climb, a clear apogee and a steady parachute descent.

What I would change

The report’s recommendations are to calibrate the MPU6050 motion sensor in the final flight code, use GPS and LoRa modules for better navigation and communication, and log timestamps with millisecond precision so the flight data is easier to process.
